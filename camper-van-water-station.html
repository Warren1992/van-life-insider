Plan a Camper Van Water Station You Can Refill Without Unpacking

Use a full-jug carry test to find a water setup that works with the bed down and the aisle clear.

Parked setup, not to scale. Cap and secure all containers before driving. Illustration: Van Life Insider

A water jug might fit neatly under the bed and still be in the wrong place. If you have to pull out the kitchen tote, fold the mattress, and turn a heavy jug sideways to get it out, you'll repeat that routine every time you refill. Before you buy a sink or drill a hole, test where the water needs to go.

Start at the door, not the cabinet

Park the van and set it up the way you would at camp. Put the bed in sleeping mode, pack the kitchen tote, and put your shoes in their usual spot. Set an empty drinking-water jug where you think it belongs. Carry it out through the door you'll actually use at the refill stop, then bring it back in. Can you set it down without stepping over anything or moving the bed?

If the route works empty, try it again at your planned fill level, only if you can lift that weight comfortably. Check whether the handle clears the doorway, whether you can grip it without reaching under the bed, and whether the jug fits under the tap you intend to use. If the carry is awkward even with a clear aisle, try a smaller container before rebuilding the layout.

Choose by lift weight, not just gallons

Water weighs about 8.34 pounds per US gallon. Five gallons is roughly 42 pounds before you count the jug. Two 2.5-gallon jugs carry the same amount, but each lift is about 21 pounds of water plus its container. The total water weight doesn't change. Two jugs can also take up more floor space and require a place to secure each one.

For a compact van, I'd start by testing a low spot just inside the sliding door, beside the aisle rather than in it. Compare it with a rear-door spot if that's the easier route. Let your trip length, refill access, and loaded vehicle's payload limit determine capacity. These jug sizes are examples, not a water allowance.

Keep drinking water and dishwater separate

Use a container intended for drinking water with a lid that closes tightly. Label it, fill it from a known potable source, and follow the CDC's safe-storage guidance. Catch dishwater in a removable wash basin, then transfer it to a separate, clearly labeled container that closes for travel. Use a different color or shape so you don't grab the wrong jug in the dark.

Check the campsite's disposal instructions before leaving. For example, Shenandoah National Park directs campers to strain basin dishwater and use designated utility sinks, while trailers and RVs use dump stations for wastewater. If there's no approved disposal point where you're staying, pack the used water out until you reach one.

Test the parked setup and the driving setup separately

A basin can sit out while you're parked. It shouldn't ride loose on a counter. Keep the jugs capped and upright and secure them and the basin before driving. Follow the vehicle and restraint manufacturers' instructions for suitable cargo anchor points, and check the load again after a refill or after transferring water to the waste jug. Don't count on a friction mat or a snug gap between boxes to hold a full container. See NHTSA's cargo-securing guidance.

Give the layout one overnight trial

  1. Before leaving: Check your refill source and disposal option. Secure everything and make sure the exits remain clear.
  2. At camp: Get a drink and wash one meal's dishes with the bed set up. Check what you had to move and whether you could pour without spilling.
  3. Before packing up: Note how much clean water is left, how much waste you collected, and the one step you'd change next time.

If the only nuisance was reaching the jug, move it before you shop for plumbing. A cabinet is a lot harder to relocate than a container.
